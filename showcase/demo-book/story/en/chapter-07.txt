For the Next Borrower

The bell at the new shop wasn't the old one.

When we took down the copper bell, its clapper broke. The hardware shop could repair it, but a part had to be ordered. We left it beside the counter and hung a clear glass bell instead.

“That isn't preserving the original sound,” I said.

“The new shop doesn't have to sound like the old one.” My aunt considered it. “When it's fixed, they can take turns.”

The key had moved from her neck to her apron pocket. It clicked against coins as she passed the half-filled shelves. Without looking up, I could tell where she was.

On Wednesday, August twelfth, Yunagi Books opened at its new address.

The sign now read WEDNESDAY CRATE OPEN. SHOP OPEN TOO. With less space for doing many things at once, Aunt Sumi would put out fewer books and take her time. The window was smaller. From the seaward stool, you could still see the harbor.

I arrived half an hour early and shelved the last poetry box. The book with the damp back cover was dry, a faint water mark still visible. I put on a clean sleeve without hiding it.

The glass bell sounded while I was crouched over a gap in the order.

Nanase stood in the doorway. Her bag was a little fuller than on her first visit. Her loafers were polished; her summer sailor uniform was freshly washed. Her straight black hair still reached her waist, but her bangs seemed a fraction shorter. I didn't quite dare ask.

“Trimmed my bangs,” she said first.

“I noticed.”

“You looked as if you were checking a printing error.”

She gave my aunt a bag of lemon cakes and showed me the confirmation email. The workshop had accepted her. Half the fees were waived; her summer-job savings would pay the rest and the journey. Maho had signed the guardian consent form and checked the workshop dormitory.

“Yokohama tomorrow?”

“Hiroshima today to stay with Mum, then on tomorrow. She won't have to answer me between handovers for once.”

“She'll have questions.”

“Already made a list. Charger, raincoat, everything. Last line: Message me when you arrive.”

She didn't sound irritated by the concern. Perhaps a little, but it could sit beside happiness.

My aunt brought out the old booklet's box. A Map for Rainy Days opened freely now, with the blue library card separately sleeved beside it. With Maho's permission we'd made a reading copy. The original remained hers. She would keep it; the crate accounts stayed at the shop. Nobody had surrendered her belongings to become somebody else's monument.

“She's collecting it herself when she comes home,” my aunt told Nanase. “And explaining in person that my sea didn't have eight legs.”

“That was my drawing.”

“I know. You'll need to be there.”

Nanase laughed into her hands before opening the last blank pages. We hadn't filled them with what happened afterward. An added sheet in the copy explained its provenance and what we'd confirmed. The original card's last box stayed empty.

“Don't you want to fill it?” I asked.

She shook her head. “It isn't my blank.”

Then she looked at the fresh cards on the counter. Paler blue, with different boxes. Borrower, title, date out, agreed return date, and a little room for renewals or notes.

She chose one of my booklets and laid it before my aunt.

“May I borrow this?”

“The author is over there.”

“Author, do you lend your books?”

“Yes. No folded corners.”

She immediately produced a plain bookmark and waved it as if she'd brought identification.

My aunt wrote the title and turned the card. “Your own name, please.”

Nanase swept her long hair behind one shoulder. The pen touched blue paper, leaving a point darker than the rule. She wrote Mio Nanase carefully, then offered me the pen and looked up with a smile.

“August twenty-sixth. After the workshop. Another Wednesday.”

“I'll be back from Osaka too.”

“You didn't withdraw?”

“No. My classmate says the table's paid for. If I quit now, he'll have to face my wrong door handles alone.”

After laughing, she filled the final line with the date and added May renew beside it.

“Advance notice. Not a disappearance.”

I wrote beneath it: For renewal, contact the author.

“Good at finding yourself work.”

After ten, customers began arriving. The boy returned his crab guide and pointed at another cover. Could he look at the shrimps? At his mother's surprise he bristled.

“I just didn't want them sneaking into the crab book. They're allowed their own book.”

Nanase leaned toward me. “He's learned to tell the difference sooner than I did.”

She didn't reach for the recorder. I didn't tell her we ought to preserve the scene. I moved a stool for the boy.

At noon we walked to the new bus stop. The hill was steeper than the harbor road. She insisted on carrying her bag herself, only pressing my share of the lemon cakes into my hand.

The electronic sign lit before the bus arrived. Automatically checking the time, I realized this departure was neither 4:10 nor 4:40.

“What?” she asked.

“Nothing. I'll have to learn the new timetable.”

She moved half a step closer to avoid the patch of sun. Both barrettes were in place, one retaining a faint old scratch.

“Ritsu.”

I turned.

“Call me Mio.”

I said yes, then felt the single word was too light.

“In recordings too?”

“You don't make recordings.”

“Might try.”

She paused, smiled, and opened the recording app on her phone, holding it toward me.

“First try, then. Not an inch from your mouth. You'll pop the microphone.”

I was more nervous than over my first door handle. Before I spoke, the bus's turning signal sounded around the corner. Suppressing a smile, she nudged the phone closer.

“Mio. Message me when you arrive.”

She stopped it, listened once, and looked down.

“I will.”

The doors opened. She boarded, tapped her travel card, and sat by a window with the bag on her knees. A strip of my booklet's spine showed. Before the bus moved she held up her new blue library card against the glass, waved, and slipped it between the pages.

I waited until the bus turned the next corner before remembering the lemon cake in my hand. Its paper bag had warmed. The cake hadn't been crushed.

Back at the shop, I shifted the bell half an inch to the right. The bicycle repairer came for an order, pointed with his foot, and told me he'd brought the new threshold shim for me to draw. I had to open my sketchbook again.

My phone chimed.

Thirteen seconds from Mio. A station announcement first, suitcase wheels passing the microphone. Then she lowered her voice and moved a little closer.

“In Hiroshima. Mum hasn't finished work, so I'm waiting in a bookshop. There's a book here only about crabs too.”

She didn't switch it off immediately. Half a second later came one more quiet line.

“Ritsu. Show me your second door handle as well.”

I set the old, wrong drawing beside the new one and sent her a photograph.

The glass bell sounded.

A girl I'd never seen peered in, taking in the shop, smaller than it looked from outside, and the wooden crate by the counter.

“Excuse me. Can I borrow these books?”

I put down my phone and took a blank blue library card from the drawer.

“You can. Which would you like first?”
