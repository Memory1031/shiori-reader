Two Kinds of Rain

On Wednesday the door was locked, but the shop was busier than ever.

Aunt Sumi took down the two highest shelves in Literature and found a drawing I'd made in primary school. I insisted it wasn't a sea but an unfinished topographical map. Standing below the ladder, Nanase asked why its coastline had eight legs.

“A difficult geographical feature.”

She burst out laughing. My aunt warned her not to shake the ladder while laughing before either of us.

After lunch we cleared the low table by the window. I spread absorbent paper and swept dust from the booklet's crevices with a soft brush. Nanase set her bag on a chair, gathered her long hair to one side, and reached for her recorder.

“May I?”

“Record book repair?”

“Yes. I won't ask questions if you don't want your voice in it.”

“You may only get my stomach.”

She carefully placed the machine at the corner farthest from my stomach.

Inside the blue cloth cover, four folded sheets nested together, sewn through the middle with pale cotton thread. The thread was brittle, but its holes were intact. I opened the difficult fold without forcing it and tested the stuck area with a narrow strip of paper.

“Can you save it?” She leaned close.

“I can help it come apart slowly. I can't make it new.”

“It doesn't need to be new. Then it wouldn't look like something Mum drew in secret.”

There was affection in the way she said Mum, as though she had finally earned the right to tease her mother a little.

Eight walking routes filled the first half. Which way the wind came over the seawall, which shop awnings offered shelter, whether a vending machine had a bin nearby. Small notes recorded anything worth knowing. The first route ended at Shiomachi Pavilion, beside an umbrella with an erased and crookedly redrawn handle.

“Mum says she couldn't do perspective.”

“Can she now?”

“Now she doesn't draw umbrellas. She just tells me to take one.”

I looked up and caught her holding back a smile. A postal motorbike passed outside, lifting a brief gust through the window. Several pages billowed. She pressed them down, the back of her hand touching my wrist.

Neither of us moved away. A moment later we both reached for the glass paperweight.

“You take it.”

“You already have.”

“Oh. Right.”

She set it on the corner rather more gently than before.

The two girls in the photograph had described the same rain differently. My aunt wrote: In heavy rain, shelter behind the fish shop. The owner won't chase you away. Maho added: He'll ask you to carry the fish boxes inside first.

One page held half a badly drawn hydrangea and the words Come back next time. They hadn't treated the booklet as a work they were obliged to finish. Even its omissions didn't seem to trouble them.

When the stuck pages finally separated, Nanase held her breath.

No letter. No signature hidden for decades. A drawing of an empty bench near the end, then clean white paper.

“Is a page missing?”

“No.” I showed her the fold. “Four sheets, sixteen pages. All the paper's here. If the last sheet had been torn out, half of it would remain, and something in the front would be affected too.”

She counted again. “So the ending hasn't fallen out.”

“The paper hasn't, at least.”

She sat back and considered the blank pages. “Then what did she mean about the last page still needing to be written?”

“Your mother?”

“A message after her shift. They'd planned an afterword when the book crate finished for the summer. Never wrote it. She doesn't know why the booklet didn't go back to the shop either.”

I inspected the inside back cover. Only one edge of the card pocket was still firmly attached. The tape at its opening was unusually thick. Nanase started to pick it with a nail; I stopped her and used a rounded bamboo tool instead.

“No death by one more try today.”

She solemnly placed both hands on her knees.

We didn't peel off the tape. I propped up the pocket's already loose edge. The yellowed layer behind the card looked like old wrapping paper added for strength. A character meaning shift or duty showed at one edge, alongside half a red rule. Angling the lamp revealed faint impressions near the top: Number Seven. The rest remained under firmly attached tape.

“A bus shift?” Nanase suggested.

“Maybe a duty rota. Too risky to take this apart now. Let's leave it.”

She took a photograph and left it alone. Turned over, the card showed another set of faint rules underneath its dates, running differently from the printed grid.

I pointed them out. She bent closer; the metal buckle on her bag strap clicked against the table. The recorder's light was still on.

“One piece of paper, two lives.”

“Saving paper. Aunt Sumi still writes stock orders on publishers' flyers.”

“Can't you let me finish saying something?”

Her glare dissolved into laughter. I laughed too, then realized how long it had been since I'd spent an afternoon like this with someone I'd only just met.

My aunt went out to deliver old magazines. My phone lit up twice at the table corner. Before I could turn it over, Nanase saw ART CLUB INDEPENDENT BOOK FAIR—SUBMISSION REMINDER. She immediately looked away.

I felt as if someone had pressed a hand against my throat. I no longer knew where to put the bamboo tool.

“You have something due too?”

“Voluntary. A few classmates are sharing a table in Osaka. Selling books we make ourselves.”

“Finished yours?”

“Not yet.”

That was a respectable way of putting it. I'd finished two and dismantled them. The remaining copy consisted of a cover and folded dummy pages—excellent evidence that I had been very busy.

She didn't tell me I could definitely do it. “May I see?”

I drew the brown envelope halfway from my bag, then pushed it back.

“Not ready to look at.”

“Understood.” She withdrew her hand. “I won't pretend I've seen it and think it's wonderful.”

Relief came first. Then, immediately, something less comfortable.

She switched off the recorder and began winding her headphone cable. Half a turn later she said, “I haven't submitted mine either. There's a summer sound workshop for high school students in Yokohama. They want three minutes of something you'd like to keep. There's a fee waiver you can apply for. I've been getting ready for nearly a month.”

“You've recorded plenty.”

“Two minutes fifty. For the last ten seconds, you're supposed to say why you chose it.” Her nail tapped the plug. “I never like it when I talk. Not my voice exactly. I sound as if I really want somebody to praise me. I don't like that.”

“When I look at other people's books, I start counting everything missing from mine.”

She looked up. I took out the envelope completely, though I kept its flap shut.

“Would you keep recording if you didn't go?” I asked.

“Yes.” No hesitation.

“Then you don't only like it for the praise.”

She fell silent. After a while she touched the closed envelope.

“Would you keep folding paper and drawing and making things if you didn't go to the fair?”

I looked down.

“Yes.”

She didn't turn that into a lesson. She moved the paperweight between us, giving the conversation somewhere it could rest.

By evening, the booklet's pages turned slowly but freely. I tied the loose thread in a knot that could be undone rather than replacing it. Beside me, Nanase wrote a label in much tidier handwriting than her mother's: DRYING. NO HEAVY OBJECTS.

After sticking it on the storage box, she asked if I'd help with an interview.

“Not suddenly shove a microphone at Sumi. I'll write questions first. She can read them and refuse anything she doesn't want to answer.”

I said it was a good idea. Her eyes brightened as she put the recorder away. I didn't ask how she intended to introduce the old story. Nor did I think again about her words only one girl.

I thought that since all the paper was there, the rest would gradually fall into place.

The wooden crate remained open under the counter. Empty. It offered no such assurance.
