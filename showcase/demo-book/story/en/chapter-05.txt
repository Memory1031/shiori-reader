A Line, Not a Debt

On Thursday Nanase came without her recorder.

I was drawing a door handle at the counter. An enormous hand gripped a tiny brass ring. It looked as if someone was trying to dismantle the door.

“Drew your first picture?”

“Fifth. The first one's underneath.”

“Hidden it?”

“Stacked it. Not destroyed it.”

She inspected the corner I showed her and provisionally accepted my explanation. Then she went upstairs to help my aunt with the accounts. Moving day was close enough for even the hardest things to sort to have their turn.

Upstairs was stifling. When the fan swung toward her, Nanase pinned her billowing skirt beneath her knees and freed a hand to push a pile of notebooks at me.

“Nineteen ninety-seven to two thousand. Under the biscuit tin. I nearly used them to support a box.”

I knew that tin. The answer had gone upstairs in my hands two days before, tucked beneath it.

Between orders and consignment lists were thin loose forms. My aunt leafed through them in a chair against the wall while we sat at opposite edges of a rush mat. In the third notebook, Nanase made a quiet sound.

A boat had been drawn at the top of the form.

I didn't connect it with the photograph until she set the blue library card's picture beside it. The vertical stroke in One person bent slightly left at the bottom, as if the writer always gave his wrist an extra turn. The card had the same bend.

Aunt Sumi took the form. “My father.”

“You didn't recognize it before?”

“I usually saw him writing the shop's name, or book titles. Not these hurried little notes.” She pointed. “But that's definitely his ‘returned.’ He always left a stroke out of that character.”

The heading was perfectly clear: Wednesday book crates—loans, staffing, returns to stock. One column counted people, two recorded times, and the last required a return signature.

I enlarged the seven at the top of the card. The missing section would have had room for the character for crate. The narrow printed boxes weren't a special form at all. They belonged to a used library card.

“Crate number seven.”

Nanase turned it over. Through the loosened edge of the pocket, the character for duty became unmistakable. Its red rule ran the same way as the staffing line on the form.

My aunt slowly retrieved the memory. “A card went out with each crate. Who took it, when it came back. Dad got fed up with our messy records and wrote instructions on the back. Someone always had to get back to cover the shop.”

What we'd taken for a book's borrowing history had recorded the crate itself. Each Wednesday it left the shop and returned around 4:40. After the 4:10 bus departed, there were fewer readers by the pavilion. That was when the girls could pack up.

I remembered the harbor timetable. It hadn't lost a bus. We'd insisted on finding someone waiting where there wasn't a bus to wait for.

“Who was the one person in the last entry?”

My aunt opened the shop diary for that August. Granddad had recorded weather, sales, even a repair to his daughter's cartwheel. On August fifth, beneath the crate entry, was a short Sumi.

“Me. Your mother went early to settle into her dormitory. I wheeled the crate back on my own. Dad received it. He probably put ‘one person’ where the signature should go to remind me to claim my hours.”

“Hours?”

“He paid us. Complained about the crate of shells, but never docked our wages.”

Her thumb rested on the page. Eventually she smiled. “I used to think he disliked the whole business. He even kept track of how often he'd oiled the wheels.”

Nanase closed the photograph on her phone and looked directly at the accounts between us.

My aunt found another page. At the foot was a failed supper: Salted mackerel. Burned one. Bought Sumi a boxed meal. When I was small, Granddad already cooked very little. He kept sweets in the highest cupboard so I couldn't find them the moment I arrived. Only now did I learn he could burn his daughter's dinner and eat the ruined fish himself.

“He wrote that down?”

“For himself. So he'd remember the heat next time. Anything not paid from shop money went down here.” She showed us rice, a replacement bicycle light, and a crooked note: Don't let her stand on the stool for books again.

Nanase pointed downstairs. “You were on a ladder yesterday.”

“Someone held it yesterday.”

I nodded. I began to see why she kept these notebooks. The figures hadn't turned into beautiful sentences for us to remember. A fish still burned. A wheel still needed repair. She didn't linger over the page, but she kept a thumb beneath its brittle edge so it wouldn't fold.

The blue library card's empty final box hadn't even been intended as a mystery. Granddad had added his note and put it into the booklet's pocket before his daughter signed. Maho later took the book home and the card with it. Moves and clear-outs had followed until it reached our counter again.

“So nobody owes a book,” Nanase said.

“If you want borrowers in arrears, I have several downstairs. I can telephone those.”

She smiled but didn't let herself off so quickly.

“The handwriting wasn't all I got wrong.” She straightened. “I thought finding the missing person would put your past right. I didn't ask whether you thought something was missing.”

My aunt half closed the notebook, making room for her hands.

“I did think about other roads. A publisher's job after college. Leaving for even a year. Sometimes I still wonder what would have happened. That isn't the same as your mother failing to return a promise.”

Nanase nodded. “I shouldn't have written your answer into a programme before asking you. I'm sorry. I won't use that introduction.”

“Good.”

Just that. Not It doesn't matter, nor an invitation to explain herself further. Nanase placed her hands on her knees and bowed her head.

I admitted I'd read it and said nothing. My aunt gave me a sideways look. “That envelope you can't discuss—open today?”

“Open. Not full yet.”

“Fill it slowly. Don't use someone else's story for ballast.”

Before we went downstairs, Nanase asked to call her mother. No recording. She wanted to explain the card. My aunt arranged a seat with her left ear toward the phone and agreed.

Maho recognized Granddad's boat immediately. A boy used to wait there for his sister to finish cram school. He worried the crate would disappear. Granddad taught him to read the register. As long as number seven said Returned, it could be borrowed again next Wednesday.

“I'd forgotten,” my aunt said.

“You were busy fixing the cloth cover. And telling me my umbrella was backwards.”

“It was backwards.”

For half a minute they argued over umbrella ribs. Nanase sat beside them, at last laughing as a daughter who didn't have to host the programme.

Her mother asked about the workshop.

“Not submitted. Sunday's the deadline.”

“Worried about the waiver? If you don't get it, we'll talk. Finish what you want to make first.”

Nanase opened her mouth. This time the familiar Almost done didn't come out.

“I'm afraid I'll submit it and they'll find I don't love it as much as I say.”

“I don't want to go to work every day either,” her mother said. “Sometimes after nights, I just want sleep, not to be someone who chose the right life. You can take a two-week class. You needn't give an answer for yourself twenty years from now.”

Nanase lowered her head and pressed a fingertip firmly against a corner of the phone, as if the words might slide off the table if she didn't.

That afternoon we put the card in an acid-free sleeve. We didn't fill the final box or peel any more tape. The accounts confirmed what the instructions meant. Reading every stroke wasn't worth tearing it.

Nanase wrote a new label: Wednesday Book Crate No. 7—Handover Card, 1998.

“What about the final page?” I asked. “The unwritten afterword.”

She looked at A Map for Rainy Days. “They didn't write it. We don't need to use what we know today to pretend they knew it then.”

Aunt Sumi rolled the crate to the door and tested the wheels.

“Free next Wednesday?”

Nanase looked at me; I looked at her. Neither answered for the other.

“In the afternoon,” she said.

“Me too.”

“Let's lend it once more.” My aunt brushed dust off the rim. “The pavilion's under construction. We'll use the old shop doorway. If it rains, indoors. Whatever we can actually manage now.”

Nanase knelt to steady a sticking wheel. She didn't ask whether this counted as an ending. She asked for a screwdriver.
