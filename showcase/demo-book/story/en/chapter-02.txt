No Bus at Four Forty

Nanase was eleven minutes early the next morning.

When I reached the shop, she was under the awning fitting a wind muff over the recorder. Gray fur made the little machine resemble an animal fresh from sleep. Holding it by both sides, she inspected it as seriously as if she were checking its teeth.

“Only a light breeze today. We've reached an understanding.”

Aunt Sumi handed us two bottles of barley tea. “They're still working at the pavilion. Don't climb the fence. Come back for lunch even if you find nothing.”

“We're investigating, not excavating,” Nanase promised.

“I'm worried you'll make the investigation sound so reasonable.”

She cooled her cheek against a bottle and wisely let that pass.

In late July, Shiomicho sloped gently from the railway station to the sea. Bicycle brakes reached corners before their riders. Bedding aired on an upstairs railing covered half a window. We passed the neighborhood hall with its summer-festival notice and turned toward the old harbor by the shrine steps. The pavilion's gray-green roof was visible inside protective netting, one curved corner peeking free.

Nanase stopped four times. Wind chimes first, then a bicycle crossing a puddle, then a ferry's horn. At the fourth stop she handed me her headphones.

“Listen.”

A busy scraping, then a click so close it seemed someone had snipped a tiny piece of paper beside my ear.

“An awning?”

She pointed at a closed barber's. A plastic lucky cat at the window kept tapping the blind with its raised paw.

“Working hard to bring people in.”

I listened again. The cat didn't know its owner had put CLOSED TUESDAYS on the door.

Shiomachi Pavilion hadn't originally been a bus stop. People sat there to wait for boats, shelter from rain, or watch the tide. When buses began stopping nearby, they borrowed its name. Once passenger boats stopped using the old harbor, the bus route shifted inland. Inside the fencing, neither pavilion nor sign had been demolished. Rotten benches were being replaced and the accessible ramp relaid.

Nanase switched off her recorder.

“Not recording?”

“I called the supervisor listed on the notice. No recording their conversations while they're working.” She put it away. “Sometimes I ask in advance.”

She was answering my aunt's remark. I took care not to laugh.

A footpath remained outside the fence. Through the wire we could see the green bus-stop pole, its old timetable trapped under cracked plastic. A newer notice in one corner gave the year the route had closed. Nanase enlarged the photograph she'd taken of the card.

“Nineteen ninety-eight. Mum was eighteen.”

I checked a calendar on my phone. July 1, 8, and 15 were indeed Wednesdays. So were the 22nd and 29th. The final entry was August 5, with One person. Returned squeezed beside it.

“August fifth is a Wednesday this year too,” she said. At my look, she added, “Calendars repeat. I'm not claiming punctual ghosts.”

The last bus we could make out on the sign was 4:10 p.m. Before that, 3:05 and 1:20. Nothing at 4:40.

“Could the timetable have changed?”

“I asked yesterday.” She opened a reply from the town's transport office. “They attached the schedule kept when the route closed. Anything earlier needs the town histories, but that part had always been four ten. The driver had to get to the other end for the schoolchildren.”

“You'd done half the job before inviting me.”

“The half you can do through a phone.” She pointed at the pavilion. “The phone couldn't smell the rest for us.”

Wind threaded the wooden slats: damp timber, dry seaweed, a little rustproofing paint. On the landward pillar were several uneven horizontal marks. Remembering the grooves on the crate, I crouched to inspect them. Only paint scraped away when a bench had been moved.

She didn't laugh. She crouched too and studied those rather useless marks with me.

“At least we haven't found another code.”

At the municipal library's local-history room, the librarian didn't produce a clipping that solved everything. She pointed at the bottom two albums of street photographs. Nanase washed her hands before touching their cloth covers.

In one picture, the pavilion had no fence. A wheeled wooden crate stood before a bench with two girls beside it. One held an umbrella, laughing with her eyes closed. The other hugged a stack of books and looked beyond the camera, a hair elastic between her teeth.

“Mum.” Nanase touched the umbrella girl's image.

“And Aunt Sumi.”

The caption said only: Wednesday's seaside book crate. The photograph had been taken late that July.

Her phone vibrated. She went outside to answer. Through the glass I watched the investigator gradually become a daughter hoping her mother wouldn't ask whether she'd eaten lunch.

She returned, put the phone on the desk, and turned the speaker very low.

“Mum, Fujiwara's here too. Sumi's nephew.”

Maho Nanase greeted me, then said to her daughter, “Sumi was better with children. One boy thought you had to write your name to borrow a book. He couldn't write his surname, so she let him draw a boat. Your grand—Sumi's father copied the boat into his notebook so we wouldn't lose it.”

“Why did you stop?”

“I went to nursing school in Hiroshima. Her father fell off a ladder that summer and had his hand in plaster. The shop was busy. After that, we had other things to do.”

“Did you promise her you'd come back?”

A light sound, a plastic cup being set down.

“Of course. I didn't ask her to wait in the pavilion forever. I've been back plenty of times. You visited the shop with me when you were little. Why?”

“The last line says ‘One person. Returned.’ I wondered—”

“I honestly don't remember that bit,” her mother gently interrupted. “Ask her. But don't start by making me someone who ran away. Nursing school was my choice. Sumi gave me a bag of plum sweets for the journey and told me not to eat them all at once.”

Nanase scraped the desk edge with a fingernail and murmured assent. Someone called Maho's name. She promised more photographs after her shift and hung up.

We put the album away and went back into the fierce sunlight. Only two red-bean daifuku remained at the little sweet shop. Nanase deliberated, then chose the smaller one.

“You prefer the small ones?”

“The bigger one looks more likely to keep a box mover alive until evening.”

I pushed both toward the shopkeeper and asked her to halve them. The sticky rice dough clung to the wooden knife. Laughing, she told us to give her a moment while she changed it.

We ate on a low harbor wall. Nanase held a larger half in its paper. A trace of white flour remained by her mouth, unnoticed. I offered a tissue.

“Do you think I'd already decided how the story ended?” she asked.

“A little. Two girls, one umbrella. Then only one girl.”

She didn't argue. She shrank the photograph on her phone, then enlarged it again.

“Mum always seems so certain. What school, which job, when she's on nights. Hearing about her at eighteen, I wanted to know if she ever didn't know what to do.”

I ate without rushing to answer.

“And she says she did know where she wanted to go.” A small smile. “It's unfair of me to be disappointed, isn't it?”

“You can be disappointed first. Just don't change her answer for her.”

She glanced at me and offered the rest of her daifuku. “Same to you. You were changing that crate's answer yesterday.”

She didn't press Record on the way back. Passing the barber's, she paused to listen to the lucky cat tapping the blind, then simply raised a hand to it.

At 4:40 Aunt Sumi put our empty tea bottles into the recycling. She pulled out the old crate and lifted a stack of receipts.

“Find anyone?”

“We've eliminated a bus,” Nanase said.

Once she caught the words, my aunt laughed. She gave me the biscuit tin of receipts and told me to take it upstairs to the box marked OLD ACCOUNTS—KEEP.

I didn't yet know the answer would get upstairs before we did.
