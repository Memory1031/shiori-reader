The Wednesday Borrower

The first time I met Mio Nanase, I was trying to drag a box marked POETRY—HANDLE WITH CARE down the stairs.

The bottom of the box objected first.

There was a brief tearing sound. Six poetry collections slid toward the door. Still clutching the surviving half of the box, I watched a thin volume stop at a pair of dark brown loafers. The shoes had avoided the puddle outside. The book hadn't quite managed it.

“Do you need a reader to catch those, or a reader to pretend she didn't see?”

“Catch them first. You can apply to forget afterward.”

She knelt to pick them up, gathering her waist-length black hair behind one shoulder. Her white sailor blouse had a navy collar and ribbon; she tucked her knee-length skirt neatly around her legs. Standing again, she barely cleared the hundred-and-fifty-centimeter mark beside the counter. Two slender ivory barrettes sat at her temple like a pair of lines someone had forgotten to erase.

She put the books on the counter and checked the damp one. Only a corner of its back cover had touched the water.

“You're Fujiwara?”

“If you're here for a refund from the moving company, no.”

“Sumi said her nephew was helping. She didn't mention he released poetry into the wild.”

My aunt laughed from behind a shelf. She was on the floor wrapping an enamel cup in newspaper. “Mio, good timing. Introduce yourselves. I won't rush in and make excuses for him.”

Boxes covered the floor of Yunagi Books. Every other step brought you to something whose fate remained undecided: a chair without a back, half a bag of button batteries, the faded umbrella stand. The landlord needed to repair the roof. We had to leave by the Sunday after next. The new shop was only a street away, with a sea-facing window half the size of this one. Aunt Sumi was already wearing its key around her neck.

“I'm Mio Nanase. Not a moving company, and I'm not here to buy anything.” She swung her canvas bag around. “I wanted to ask about a library card.”

She drew a piece of blue card from a clear folder. Wider than a bookmark, it had corners worn pale gray and a notch missing from the top right. Where a title should have been, only a handwritten seven remained. Beneath it were small boxes for dates, times, and signatures. The final box was empty, although someone had added a note beside it: One person. Returned.

The earlier entries were stranger. July 1, 4:40 p.m. July 8, 4:40 p.m. July 15, 4:40 p.m.

“All Wednesdays,” Nanase said.

The wooden sign by the door still read CLOSED WEDNESDAYS. All three of us looked at it, as if it might be called upon to testify.

Aunt Sumi got up and put the cup on a box. She turned slightly to bring her left ear toward us; otherwise she sometimes missed what came next.

“Where did you find it?”

“Mum asked me to sort her old things. Keep what's useful, throw away the rest. I didn't think I should be the one deciding whether this was useful.”

My aunt traced the edge. “Your mother and I used to run a Wednesday book crate. We'd wheel books from the shop down to Shiomachi Pavilion. Before she went away to study.”

“So the shop took a day off, but you didn't?” I asked.

“There weren't that many customers here. Your grandfather complained that we came back with fewer books and more stones. Eventually he made us a little cart.”

Granddad had been dead for three years. His shelves and badly made stools remained in the shop. I had always known they were sturdy. It hadn't occurred to me to ask whether they had stories.

“And this—‘One person. Returned’?” Nanase pointed. “Did one of you write that?”

My aunt held the card against the window. Light showed another yellowed layer stuck to the back, with a narrow strip of tape obscuring some writing.

“The times are mine. I'm not sure about this part. It almost looks like your mother's hand, but not quite.” She put it down without pretending to remember. “Nearly twenty-eight years. Some things I remember. Some I'd need the accounts to be sure of.”

“Mum thought it belonged in that booklet you made. I found the booklet. I couldn't find a person to go with the empty space.”

Nanase produced a small stitched book. Two shades of blue cloth formed its cover. The title was A Map for Rainy Days. Its edges had softened in some long-ago downpour. The last few pages were stuck together. The card had been inside a paper pocket at the back; the pocket was coming loose.

Aunt Sumi looked at it for a long time, then brushed a speck of dust off the cloth.

“So it ended up at your house.”

“Mum says she took two copies. No idea where the other one is.”

“I lost mine years ago.” My aunt handed the booklet to me. “It wasn't published. Just routes we drew. The seawall, the old bus station, the rice-cake stall—anything we liked. My drawings were bad. Your mother's handwriting was worse.”

“She told me to ask if you'd stand by the second half of that statement.”

My aunt laughed. This was a different smile from the one she wore over moving lists.

“Don't force it open, Ritsu. The paper will break. Didn't you learn a little book repair? See if you can make it turn properly again.”

I was a second-year high school student in Kobe, seventeen like her. Our art club had taught us a little bookmaking, not conservation. In those sessions, I had been best at putting the blade safely back in its case and worst at explaining my own work. Correcting my aunt at a time like this usually earned me more jobs.

“I'll look first. I won't force a repair if it isn't safe.”

Nanase pushed the folder toward me. “Please stick to that. Mum says all my broken toys died of ‘one more try should do it.’”

She spoke quickly but kept a hand on the booklet. I noticed blue ink beside one fingernail.

“Does this book mean a lot to you?”

“I'm working on a sound piece.” She indicated the window. Across the street, the old shelter stood behind construction fencing. “Recording places that are changing. A few years from now, when someone says there used to be a pavilion here, I'd like them to be able to hear it.”

She took out a portable recorder and played a sample. Footsteps on stone. Someone calling that the bean daifuku would be gone soon. Then wind thumping against the microphone.

“The wind wanted a solo.” She stopped it. “The machine's fine. The operator wasn't respectful enough.”

“Will the card be in the piece too?” my aunt asked.

“If I can find the last person. I want to know who kept borrowing books when the shop was closed.”

My aunt tucked the new key inside her shirt. “Record what you find. Don't invent somebody who never came just because it sounds better.”

Nanase nodded and typed that into her phone. Later I would learn that writing something down and taking it in were different activities.

At noon my aunt went out for packing tape. Nanase stayed to help lay out the poetry books, slipping clean paper between them. Her hands moved slowly, as if they belonged to someone quite different from her voice.

A wooden crate stuck out from under the counter. I pulled it free. Seven uneven grooves marked its side. She knelt beside me and ran a finger over the deepest.

“Number seven?”

“Or perhaps Aunt Sumi stored potatoes in it seven times.”

She looked at me. I surrendered and turned the crate over. Nothing underneath except a circular paper label faded white.

“Are you afraid people will realize you're serious?” she asked.

“What?”

“Whenever something might be true, you offer an explanation that couldn't be. It's fine to guess wrong. I won't keep a record.”

I had another joke ready. Suddenly I couldn't find anywhere to put it.

She didn't press. She held the blue library card in her palm and examined its empty final box. A cloud moved outside. The paper brightened like a little patch of shallow sea pressed flat.

“What if the thing they returned wasn't a book?”

“Then we need to find out what the card was for.”

She looked up with a small, serious delight in her eyes.

“Come to the pavilion tomorrow. You read the paper. I'll ask directions.”

I agreed, then glanced at the boxes still stranded on the stairs.

“Before that, you may need to help six poets return to Literature.”

“Didn't you say they'd been released?”

She picked up the top stack, leaving only the damp volume. We placed it beside the window under a clean, empty cup. An old tea stain marked the enamel. Afternoon light passed its rim and settled in the last line on the blue library card.
