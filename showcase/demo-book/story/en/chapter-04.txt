Don't Call It My Regret

The interview outline was only two pages long.

Seven questions on the first. A title and short introduction on the second. Later I often thought how we should have started with page one—and how we'd put page two on top instead.

The One Who Stayed on Wednesday.

Beneath it, Nanase had written: Twenty-eight years ago, one girl left town. The other stayed by the sea, keeping an unfinished map. The sunshine they lent still awaits its final return.

I thought the sentences were lovely. Lovely enough not to stop and ask whether they were true.

Aunt Sumi was oiling boards for the new shop when she read the outline. She rested the brush safely, took off her glasses, and read both sheets with care before setting them aside.

Nanase sat straight-backed. The recorder stayed inside her bag. Her knees pressed the edge of the stool, one foot tucked inward.

“That bit can change,” she said before my aunt spoke. “It's only a way in. I don't have to read it exactly.”

“Why do you think I was waiting for somebody?”

Nanase blinked.

“Not literally all this time. The booklet, that last line on the card—”

“We haven't worked that line out. Your mother went to study. I worked here. You asked about those things. How did writing them down turn it into one girl leaving and one being left behind?”

Her voice wasn't loud. She moved the oil tin away so it wouldn't spill on the paper.

“I wasn't trying to make you sound pitiful.”

“I know. But I don't want to sound admirable either.”

She looked at the half-empty shelves. Pale outlines remained where boards had been, making the wall seem more like a bookshop than when books filled it.

“Your grandfather hurt his hand. I did think I'd stay and help. His hand healed. I stayed because I liked the work. Someone comes in at a change of season asking for a book whose title they can't remember, and I can find it—that makes a good day. There are bad ones. Wrong orders, losses, days when I'd like to move the entire street somewhere else. None of that was for waiting for your mother.”

I started to say Nanase hadn't meant it like that. My aunt looked at me.

“Ritsu, do you think this version sounds better too?”

I couldn't answer.

She returned the outline. “You may read the old book. Ask before using anything I've said. But I won't do this interview for now.”

The paper trembled a little in Nanase's hands.

“I'm sorry.”

“Don't rush to apologize. Think about what you want to ask. Then come back.”

My aunt replaced her glasses and picked up the brush. Half the board still needed oiling. Her work didn't stop because we had gone quiet.

I followed Nanase to the door. Fine rain had begun outside, rustling on the awning like someone continuously crumpling an enormous sheet of thin paper.

“You don't have to walk me home.”

“I'm not here to explain what she meant.”

She stopped and looked up. “Then what are you going to say?”

“I read it too. I liked the introduction. I didn't warn you.”

“It isn't too late now.” She shook her head. “No. That sounds like asking you for a version that didn't happen.”

She slid the folder into her bag. A corner caught in the zip. Instead of pulling harder, she freed it and laid it flat.

“I'll rewrite it.”

“It's raining.”

“I brought an umbrella.”

One rib refused to straighten. I helped ease it into place. She said a small thank-you. The rain swallowed most of it, but not all.

After she left, my aunt had me move the boards somewhere airy. On my third trip I lingered at the door.

“Are you angry?”

“A little.”

“She didn't—”

“Ritsu.”

I stopped.

She washed the brush, whitening the water in its bowl. “She can make a mistake and deal with it herself. You needn't translate what she did into something easier to forgive.”

I rubbed an oil mark on my hand with my thumb, only making it larger.

“You too,” she added. “If you don't want to answer, say so. Don't always wait for someone to ask whether you're busy.”

In the bag under the counter, my booklet of blank dummy pages remained silent. Nanase, at least, had handed over something that could be rejected. I was avoiding even that.

Toward evening the rain grew heavier. I carried wrapping paper to the window and saw her outside the bakery opposite. She hadn't noticed me. She was staring at its opening hours.

I messaged to ask if she was still out.

Waiting until I stop wanting to prove I'm not a bad person, she replied.

Then: Also waiting for the salt rolls.

I showed my aunt. She pointed at the window table. “Let her wait in here. You can fetch the bread when it's ready.”

Nanase said nothing about interviews when she came in. She put her umbrella in the stand.

“May I sit for a while? No recording.”

“Of course. By the window. There's a lamp.”

She settled at yesterday's repair table, bag by her feet, phone facedown beside her draft. Rain threaded the glass. Harbor lights came on. The warm light on her white blouse stayed the same.

I fetched three salt rolls. When I returned, she was supporting her cheek with one hand and holding the title sheet in the other. The introduction was crossed out. Below it were several short questions.

Who else read the booklet?

Would you like to run the Wednesday crate again?

What may I record, and what shouldn't I?

Seeing me, she shifted the paper toward herself. I stopped reading.

“Have I made the application too complicated?” she asked, warming her hands around the bread.

“Possibly. They asked for three minutes.”

She let out a tired little laugh. “I wanted a beautiful story. As if, once everyone in it could be understood, my own ten seconds wouldn't matter so much.”

I caught crumbs in a tissue. “Keeping my book a dummy helps too. Everyone knows it could still get better.”

“Are we both very good at leaving an escape route?”

“Speak for yourself. Mine has six boxes of books in it.”

This time she didn't meet my joke.

“I don't want Sumi to be material for my application.” She set the bread down. “But I think that's what I just made her.”

All I could say was, “Tell her yourself tomorrow.”

“And you?”

“I'll open the envelope.”

She looked at me, testing whether that was another respectable phrase I wouldn't live up to. Finally she pushed a pencil across.

“Write it down. For yourself. I won't look.”

I cut a strip from the wrapping paper and wrote: Tomorrow, draw the first picture. No changing the sheet.

The writing was ugly. I didn't rewrite it.

When my aunt came to collect the cups, Nanase stood.

“May I help tomorrow? Just moving books. I'll think about the interview before asking again.”

Aunt Sumi inspected the crumbs on her sleeve.

“Yes. Don't wear your favorite clothes. Dusty upstairs.”

“This blouse's already had book paste on it.”

“Perfect, then.”

Nanase sat down and quietly brushed her sleeve. Her shoulders finally loosened. I slipped the promise into my booklet. Outside the rain continued, but all three paper bread bags were empty.
