The Rain Won't Speak for Us

On Sunday, August second, Nanase submitted her application forty-seven minutes before the deadline.

She sent a screenshot, then: My last ten seconds lasted eleven. Wonder if they deduct marks.

I replied: You could remove one tap of the lucky cat's paw.

Much later, an audio file arrived. A page turned. Then her voice.

“I'm Mio Nanase. I'd like to learn to record these small sounds clearly. Including my own.”

A half-breath she hadn't caught and a tiny “Oh. That's done.”

On the second listen I realized I shouldn't answer with another eleven-seconds joke. I typed: Heard you clearly.

Good, she replied.

Wednesday's crate event had nothing to do with the piece she'd submitted. My aunt permitted recording, provided Nanase asked everyone who might be heard. No permission, no recording. No recording first and saying nobody would recognize them anyway.

Nanase put that at the top of her new outline. The application was finished. She no longer needed to take anything from the event, yet she prepared more carefully than before.

I fixed the sticking wheel. The seven grooves on the crate weren't a number. Granddad had tested a saw blade there. He'd numbered his crates with circular labels underneath. The label had faded white, but his accounts still recorded buying them.

“Your potatoes weren't a total defeat,” Nanase consoled me.

“We needn't put that in the event record.”

She capped her pen with regret.

On August fifth we set two low stools and a folding table outside Yunagi Books. My sign said WEDNESDAY BOOK CRATE. FREE LOANS. ASK THE BOOKSELLER TO REGISTER. Below it: RAIN MEANS INDOORS.

She considered it carefully and pointed at the last line. “Today's most reliable arrangement.”

Fewer people came than we'd imagined. The bicycle repairer brought two reference books his son had finished. The baker asked whether she should save us salt rolls. A primary-school boy in blue rain boots examined three books before realizing none were comics. Then he earnestly asked for a book only about crabs.

“What do you mean, only crabs?” my aunt asked.

“No shrimps. The last one changed to shrimps halfway through.”

She fetched an old field guide and showed him the contents. He approved the number of crab pages and decided to borrow it.

Nanase asked him and his mother if she could record the turning pages. He wanted to know whether she'd catch him saying he hated shrimps. Assured she wouldn't, he agreed.

She waited for a gust to pass before pressing Record.

At the second page he stopped. “Turn it now?”

“Whenever you like.”

He turned three pages very forcefully, then asked if that was enough. She stopped the machine, thought a moment, and moved it out of his immediate sight.

“I didn't explain properly. You don't need to turn pages for me. Find the crabs you want. I'll wait. It's all right if there isn't a useful sound.”

He forgot about her. Pages turned quickly, then slowly. Once he studied a picture for a full two minutes. She crouched there for both. Getting up, she secretly shook her numb calf, then pretended to be perfectly comfortable when she caught me looking.

I pushed a stool behind her. Finally abandoning the act, she sat and reached out a hand. I offered the recorder. She'd wanted the wet wipe I'd just opened. She laughed behind her hand until even the serious crab reader almost looked up.

Her skirt was gathered carefully over her knees. Her barrettes caught the light. For the first time I understood that what she loved wasn't wandering around with a machine looking for interesting things. It was being willing to crouch down and wait.

My aunt gave the boy a new library card, wrote the title, and indicated the signature box. He held the pen as if preparing to fight it. Having written his name, he asked if tomorrow was too soon to return the book.

“Come back when you've read it. You can renew it if you haven't.”

She said it so ordinarily that I stood there a while before fetching the essay collection box.

A little after three, rain came earlier than forecast.

One drop landed on Nanase's shoulder. She looked up, put away the recorder, and closed the open book on the table edge. As the drops multiplied, we obeyed our sign, moving stools, table, and crate indoors.

I got the folding table stuck in the doorway and had to retreat. She didn't dash out for the last stack. She waited under the awning for me to clear the door, then took the box my aunt had already covered with waterproof cloth.

“Wrong order,” she panted. “The table was smaller than the door when we brought it out.”

“It grew while it was outside.”

“Then you can take responsibility for its education.”

We laughed breathlessly. Rain polished the paving stones. One corner of the farthest flyer got wet. The books didn't.

Readers found seats beside boxes. The boy continued with his crabs, occasionally showing his mother a picture. The baker's rolls went at the far end of the counter on clean paper. My aunt forbade bread near books.

Nanase explained the recording area to those who'd agreed, then sat by the window without more questions. Gradually the shop held only turning pages, rain, and the slight scrape of someone making room with a chair.

My brown envelope stood beneath the counter. It held eight stitched booklets now. The Other Side of the Door. I'd drawn doors along the walk to the shop and the things overlooked beside them: a rusty bicycle bell, the lucky cat's blind, striped socks never hung straight.

They weren't beautiful enough. A black line had smudged on one page, almost identically in all eight copies. I'd printed replacements that morning and ended up not using them.

Nanase spotted the envelope and tipped her chin toward it. She didn't take it out for me.

After a while, I set the booklets on the table.

The bicycle repairer stopped on page three. “My front door?”

My stomach dropped. I should have asked the door's owner too.

“I left out the shop name. If you'd rather—”

“Wrong shim on the threshold. I've replaced it. Come and draw the new one sometime.”

Nanase turned away to laugh. Finally I laughed too and carefully wrote a note: Bicycle shop threshold. Ask before drawing.

He bought a copy. When I refused payment, he objected. I'd written the price plainly; surely it didn't stop counting when my first customer arrived. He put five hundred yen on the table and asked where the author's name was.

I'd printed Ritsu Fujiwara in tiny letters on the final page. Unable to find it, he let me show him. Nanase read it along with him.

“Ritsu.”

I answered before realizing she'd dropped Fujiwara.

She noticed my pause. “You don't like being called that?”

“No—I mean, it's fine.”

“Good. Your name's easier to draw than a door handle.”

“Are you saying the drawing's bad?”

“Haven't finished looking. No premature reviews.”

The rain stopped after four. We wheeled the crate back beneath the awning without the table, leaving room to stand together. Nanase showed the old card in its transparent sleeve to the last readers. No story about waiting twenty-eight years. Only that it had once tracked a wooden crate's comings and goings.

“Do we use it today?” the boy asked.

“A new one today.” She pointed. “Your crabs are already on this card.”

At 4:40 my aunt called us in.

I steadied the crate inside while Nanase fitted a last stack into it from the doorstep. Sun broke through the rain clouds onto her white blouse and the ends of her hair. Turning toward me, she raised the blue sleeve she hadn't put down and smiled with uncomplicated relief.

“Two people. Returned.”

My aunt looked up behind the counter. “You left out the bookseller.”

“Three,” Nanase promptly corrected herself. “We can't remove people for symmetry.”

We rolled the crate into place. This time its wheel didn't catch on the threshold.
